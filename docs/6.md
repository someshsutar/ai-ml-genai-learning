### What is Learning Rate in Machine Learning?

The **learning rate** controls **how much the model changes its weights during each training step**.

It is usually represented by:

$$
\boxed{\alpha}
$$

or sometimes:

$$
\boxed{\eta}
$$

It is one of the most important **hyperparameters** in ML.

---

### 1. Remember our gradient example

Previously, we calculated:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=-16
$$

Our current weight was:

$$
w=3
$$

The gradient descent formula is:

$$
\boxed{w_{new}=w_{old}-\alpha \times gradient}
$$

Suppose the learning rate is:

$$
\alpha=0.1
$$

Then:

$$
w_{new}=3-(0.1\times-16)
$$

$$
=3+1.6
$$

$$
\boxed{w_{new}=4.6}
$$

So the **learning rate determines how big that 1.6 step is**.

---

## 2. What happens if the learning rate is small?

Suppose:

$$
\alpha=0.01
$$

Then:

$$
w_{new}=3-(0.01\times-16)
$$

$$
=3+0.16
$$

$$
\boxed{w_{new}=3.16}
$$

The model moves slowly.

```text
Weight

3.00
  ↓
3.16
  ↓
3.30
  ↓
3.45
  ↓
...
```

### Advantage

More controlled learning.

### Disadvantage

Training can take a very long time.

---

## 3. What happens if the learning rate is large?

Suppose:

$$
\alpha=1
$$

Then:

$$
w_{new}=3-(1\times-16)
$$

$$
=19
$$

That's a **huge jump**.

The model may jump past the optimal value.

```text
Loss
 ^
 |       *
 |      / \
 |     /   \
 |    /     \
 | * /       \ *
 |/             \
 +------------------> Weight

   ← huge jumps →
```

The model may even **oscillate or diverge instead of learning**.

---

## 4. The three important cases

| Learning Rate | Behavior                            |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Too small** | Training is very slow               |
| **Good**      | Smoothly converges toward minimum   |
| **Too large** | Overshoots, oscillates, or diverges |

## 5. Learning rate and gradient

These two concepts work together:

$$
\boxed{\text{Weight Change} = \text{Learning Rate} \times \text{Gradient}}
$$

For example:

```text
Gradient       = -16
Learning Rate  = 0.1

Weight change = -0.1 × -16
              = +1.6
```

The **gradient tells us the direction and sensitivity**.

The **learning rate tells us how big a step to take**.

That's a very useful distinction:

> **Gradient = Which direction?**
> **Learning rate = How big a step?**

---

## 6. Learning rate in neural networks

For a neural network, we have potentially thousands or millions of weights.

During training:

```text
Input
  ↓
Neural Network
  ↓
Prediction
  ↓
Loss
  ↓
Backpropagation
  ↓
Gradients
  ↓
Learning Rate
  ↓
Weight Updates
  ↓
Next iteration
```

The basic update remains:

$$
\boxed{W_{new}=W_{old}-\alpha\nabla L}
$$

where:

* (W) = weights
* ($\nabla L$) = gradient
* ($\alpha$) = learning rate

---

### 7. Typical values

Learning rates are often relatively small numbers, such as:

```text
0.1
0.01
0.001
0.0001
```

But there is **no universally correct learning rate**. The appropriate value depends on the model, optimizer, dataset, normalization, and training setup.

---

### Simple mental model

Imagine you're searching for the bottom of a valley while blindfolded.

**Gradient:**

> "The valley slopes downward this way."

**Learning rate:**

> "Take a step of this size."

**Gradient descent:**

> "Keep taking steps downhill until you reach the bottom."

So the relationship between the concepts you've been learning is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Loss}
\rightarrow
\text{Derivative}
\rightarrow
\text{Gradient}
\rightarrow
\text{Learning Rate}
\rightarrow
\text{Weight Update}
}
$$

This is the foundation of how ML models **learn from data**.
